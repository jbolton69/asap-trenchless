Ask a Colorado structural engineer what keeps them busy and swelling soil will come up quickly. The Colorado Geological Survey describes expansive soil and rock as a leading geologic hazard in the state, and it is common across the Front Range urban corridor where most of the Denver metro's houses sit. Foundations get most of the attention. Sewer lines, buried a few feet down in the same ground, take the same punishment with far less notice.

This post explains what the soil is doing, the specific damage it causes to a lateral, and how that damage decides between lining, bursting and digging.

## What makes Front Range soil swell

Expansive soils contain clay minerals that take water into their structure and grow in volume as they do. In Colorado the usual culprits are montmorillonite and bentonite, clays formed from weathered volcanic ash that was deposited in the ancient seas and river systems that laid down much of the bedrock under the plains. According to the Colorado Geological Survey, these clays can swell by a large fraction of their dry volume and push back with enough force to crack slabs, buckle pavement and break pipe.

The climate makes it worse. Much of the Front Range is semi-arid, so the natural soil is often dry at depth. Development adds water: lawn irrigation, roof downspouts, leaking pipes and changes in drainage. Clay that was stable for thousands of years starts swelling after a house goes in. It also shrinks when it dries out again, so a single yard can see seasonal cycles of lift and settlement, sometimes different from one side of the lot to the other.

The CGS guide for Colorado homebuyers makes a point worth repeating: damage can develop slowly, and houses on the same street may show it at different times. A sewer line is no different. Two neighbors with identical 1960s laterals can have very different pipes today.

## What heave does to a sewer lateral

A sewer depends on steady downhill slope. Most residential lines are built with a modest, continuous fall from the house to the main, and waste moves because gravity carries it. Swelling clay attacks that slope in three ways.

**Offset joints.** Older laterals are built from short sections. Clay tile typically comes in short lengths joined by bell-and-spigot ends, and cast iron has hub joints. When the soil lifts one section more than its neighbor, the joint slips and the bottom of one pipe ends up above or below the next. On camera it looks like a step or a ledge. Paper, grease and solids snag there, and roots find the gap.

**Bellies.** When a stretch of pipe drops, whether from settlement, washed-out bedding or clay shrinking after heave, it forms a sag that holds water. A camera shows it clearly: the lens goes under water, then comes back up. Solids settle in the low spot, and the line clogs again and again in the same place no matter how often it is cleaned.

**Cracks and breaks.** Rigid pipe does not bend much. Clay tile, and to a lesser extent cast iron, cracks when uneven pressure loads it. Circular cracks at joints, long splits along the top or bottom of the pipe, and broken sections with missing pieces are all common findings in older Front Range laterals.

Tree roots compound all three. Every crack and open joint lets moisture out, and roots follow the moisture in.

## How a camera sorts the problem out

The first step is always a full-length [sewer camera inspection](/sewer-camera-inspection/) after the line has been cleaned, with a footage counter running and a locator marking where each defect sits on the surface. For heave damage, we are looking for three things:

- whether the pipe is still round and continuous, even if cracked;
- whether the joints have shifted, and by how much;
- whether the flow line is still going downhill the whole way, or whether water stands anywhere.

Those answers decide the method more than the age or material of the pipe does.

## When lining is the right fix

[CIPP lining](/cipp-pipe-lining/) cures a new pipe inside the old one. It is an excellent repair for a line that still holds its shape and slope: cracked clay, joints that have opened slightly, root intrusion, corroded cast iron. The liner seals the cracks and joints, stops roots and gives a smooth, jointless interior. On a heaved line with minor offsets and good grade, lining is often the least disruptive way to fix it.

What lining cannot do is change the path of the pipe. The liner follows the host pipe exactly. If there is a belly, the liner will have a belly. If a joint has dropped enough to form a real step, the liner can only smooth that step so much. We are direct about this, because a lined belly is still a belly, and the backups will come back.

## When bursting or replacement is the honest answer

**Pipe bursting** pulls a bursting head through the old line, breaking it outward and drawing a continuous fused HDPE pipe in behind it. Because the new pipe is pulled on its own tension, it can correct some offsets and restore a smoother path than the old joints allowed. It suits laterals that are too broken, deformed or offset to line but still sit on a workable grade. Read more on our [pipe bursting](/pipe-bursting/) page.

**Conventional replacement** is the answer when the grade itself has been lost, when a belly is deep or long, or when the line has to be rerouted. Digging lets the crew reset the bedding and establish a true slope, and on expansive soil that bedding matters. We still do [sewer line replacement](/sewer-line-replacement/) the traditional way when trenchless will not produce a line that works.

Often the answer is a combination: a short excavation at a sagged or collapsed section, and lining on the rest of the run.

## Reducing the stress on a line

You cannot change the clay under your yard, but you can limit the water that sets it off. Keep downspouts discharging well away from the house. Avoid heavy irrigation directly over the sewer route. Fix leaking water lines and faucets promptly. Get recurring slow drains checked before a partial clog becomes a backup into the basement. If you are in a newer subdivision, the soils report and any disclosure you received at purchase may describe the swell potential of your lot.

## Where this shows up

Swelling soil is a factor across much of the metro, from the plains of [Adams County](/adams-county/) and [Arapahoe County](/arapahoe-county/) to parts of [Jefferson County](/jefferson-county/) and [Boulder County](/boulder-county/), as well as older neighborhoods in [Denver](/denver/). The conditions vary block by block, which is why a camera, not a ZIP code, should decide the repair. For common questions about methods and permits, see our [FAQ](/faq/).
