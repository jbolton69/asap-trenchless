When a sewer backs up, the first question most people ask is whose problem it is. In the Denver metro the honest answer usually surprises them: the pipe from the house to the public main normally belongs to the property owner, even the stretch that runs under the sidewalk, the parkway strip and the street. In many places the owner is also on the hook for the fitting where that pipe joins the main.

That is not a single statewide rule. Sewer service along the Front Range is split among city utilities and dozens of special sanitation districts, and each one writes its own regulations. Below is how several of them describe the split, in their own published guidance, and what that means before you hire anyone to dig or line.

## The pieces of the system

It helps to name the parts. The **main** is the public pipe, usually down the middle of the street or an alley, that collects flow from a whole block. The **service line** (also called the lateral or building sewer) is the private pipe that carries waste from one building to that main. The **tap** or **connection** is where the two meet, typically through a fitting called a wye or a saddle.

Most disagreements happen at that last piece and in the section under the street. Owners often assume the city takes over at the property line, as it frequently does for water service. For sewer, that assumption is usually wrong.

## What the cities say

**Denver.** The City and County of Denver's wastewater FAQ says the city is responsible only for the main public sewers. If crews investigating a backup find that the problem is in the private line, the owner is told to call a plumber and is responsible for repairs and any resulting damage. For a house in Denver, the whole service line out to the main is yours. You can find more about local conditions on our [Denver page](/denver/).

**Arvada.** Arvada is more explicit. The city states that care of the sewer service line from the home up to and including the connection to the city main belongs to the homeowner. In other words, the tap itself is on the owner's side of the ledger. Some Arvada addresses are served by special districts rather than the city, so check who sends your sewer bill. Arvada sits mostly in [Jefferson County](/jefferson-county/), with a portion in Adams County.

**Brighton.** Brighton's sewer backup guidance says the city repairs the mains, while the wye that connects to the main and all of the service line from the main to the home belong to the property owner. Brighton also puts any clog or backup between the main and the house on the owner. See our [Adams County page](/adams-county/) for more on the northern suburbs.

**Longmont.** Longmont says the service line is owned and maintained by the property owner and defines it as the full length of pipe between the building and the main, noting that the main is usually in the street, an easement or an alley. Older Longmont blocks with alley mains are a reminder that "the street" is not the only place a lateral can end. Our [Boulder County page](/boulder-county/) covers Longmont and its neighbors.

**Greeley.** Greeley puts it plainly: residents are responsible for their own service line and should call a private sewer company when something goes wrong with it, and the city does not maintain private service lines. Greeley also invites prospective buyers to call its wastewater collection division to ask about a property's sewer history, which is a useful step before closing.

**Lakewood.** Lakewood states that owners maintain the entire lateral from the building to the point where it meets the main under the street. It also shows how fragmented service can be: the city itself provides sewer to only part of north Lakewood, and a variety of other providers serve the rest of the city.

## What the districts say

Special districts are where Front Range sewer rules get most varied, and where the rules are often the strictest about the connection.

**St. Vrain Sanitation District**, which serves Frederick, Firestone and Dacono, states that the service line, including the service connection, is owned by the owner of the building it serves, and that the owner is responsible for maintaining and repairing both. Its FAQ repeats the point: the line belongs to the building, not the district.

**Northwest Lakewood Sanitation District** tells customers that the owner's responsibility runs from the home to the district's main, and that the district's responsibility starts at the point of connection, usually under the street. The same page urges customers to look into insurance for sewer backups, noting that not every homeowners policy covers them.

Those two describe the tap differently, which is exactly why it pays to ask. Across [Arapahoe County](/arapahoe-county/) and the southern suburbs, a patchwork of districts serves neighborhoods that share a city name, so two houses a mile apart can live under different rules.

## How to find out which rules apply to you

1. **Look at your sewer bill.** The name at the top is the agency that owns the main you connect to. It may not be your city.
2. **Read that agency's rules on service lines.** Search its site for "service line," "lateral" or "building sewer." Look specifically for the words "including the connection" or "including the tap."
3. **Ask before you dig.** Call the city or district and ask where your responsibility ends on your street, whether they require notice before work at the main, and who inspects the connection.
4. **Check for a street-cut permit.** If the repair reaches pavement, the city or county that owns the road will usually require a right-of-way permit, separate from the plumbing permit.

## Why ownership changes the repair

Knowing the pipe is yours all the way to the main changes how you should read a quote. A spot repair near the foundation may leave the worst section, the part under the street, untouched. A replacement that stops at the property line can leave an old clay or Orangeburg section and a failing tap in place. Ask any contractor which footage of pipe the price covers, and whether it includes the connection.

It also changes the value of trenchless methods. The most expensive part of a conventional sewer replacement is often the part in the public right-of-way: saw-cutting pavement, traffic control, patching and inspections. When the pipe is a good candidate, [CIPP lining](/cipp-pipe-lining/) can renew the line all the way to the main from a cleanout, with no street cut at all. When the pipe has collapsed or lost its shape, [pipe bursting](/pipe-bursting/) can replace it using a small pit at each end rather than an open trench. And when the slope is wrong, a conventional [sewer line replacement](/sewer-line-replacement/) is still the right answer, street cut and all.

Everything starts with a [sewer camera inspection](/sewer-camera-inspection/) that runs the full length of the line, past the property line and out to the main, with the footage counter and a locator marking where each defect sits. That shows which part of the problem is on your side of the tap and which part, if any, belongs to the city or district.

## If you are buying a house

Because the lateral almost always transfers to the buyer, a scope of the sewer line before closing is one of the better inspection investments you can make on an older home. Ask for the video, not just a summary, and ask the inspector to push the camera all the way to the main if access allows. If something shows up, you will know which agency's rules apply before you negotiate. Our [FAQ](/faq/) covers more on sewer scopes, insurance and permits.
