A large share of the Denver metro's houses went up in the two or three decades after World War II. Ranches and split-levels spread across Lakewood, Arvada, Westminster, Aurora, Englewood, Littleton and Denver's outer neighborhoods. The sewer lines under those homes are often the original ones, and they were usually built from one of two materials: vitrified clay tile or bituminous fiber pipe, better known as Orangeburg.

Both can last a long time. Both also fail in predictable ways, and the way each one fails points toward a different repair. Here is what to know.

## Orangeburg: a wartime material that stuck around

Orangeburg pipe takes its name from the town in New York where it was made. According to a history published by the Minnesota chapter of the American Public Works Association, the pipe was built from layers of ground wood fiber impregnated with coal-tar pitch. It had been produced in small quantities since the late 1800s, but steel shortages during World War II and the housing boom that followed made it a popular choice for residential sewer connections. The last manufacturer closed in 1974 as plastic pipe took over.

Building departments that deal with it describe the same failure pattern. Ann Arbor, Michigan, notes that the pipe loses its round shape as it deteriorates, flattening under the weight of the soil around it, and that the inner layers can fail first so the pipe degrades from the inside out. The City of Tempe, Arizona, notes that fiber pipe was allowed under the plumbing code for decades and that it is prone to root intrusion and repeated backups.

Orangeburg was typically used for the run from the foundation to the main, not inside the house, so many homes have cast iron under the basement floor and fiber pipe once the line leaves the building. There are no reliable records of which lots got it. Only an inspection answers the question.

## Clay tile: durable pipe, weak joints

Vitrified clay is fired ceramic, and the pipe wall itself resists sewage and corrosion very well. Its weak points are the joints and its rigidity. Older clay laterals were laid in short sections with bell-and-spigot ends, and the joints were often sealed with mortar or a simple packing that has long since cracked or washed out. Those joints are where roots enter. On expansive Front Range clay, the sections also shift relative to one another, leaving offset joints and cracks. Our post on [expansive soil and sewer lines](/blog/expansive-soil-and-sewer-lines-colorado/) covers that in more detail.

## How to tell them apart on camera

A [sewer camera inspection](/sewer-camera-inspection/) after cleaning usually identifies the material quickly.

**Orangeburg on video:**

- a dark, black or brownish interior wall, sometimes with a slightly fibrous texture;
- blisters or bubbles where the inner layer has separated;
- a pipe that looks oval rather than round, sometimes flattened enough that the camera barely passes;
- no regular joints every few feet, because fiber pipe came in longer lengths;
- in advanced cases, a wall that has split, delaminated or caved in.

**Clay tile on video:**

- a reddish-brown or tan interior, often glazed and smooth;
- a joint every few feet, visible as a ring or a slight change in diameter;
- roots entering at the joints, from a few hair-like strands to a solid mass;
- circular cracks at the joints, longitudinal cracks along the pipe, and occasional missing pieces;
- offsets where one section has shifted above or below the next.

A single lateral can contain both materials, along with cast iron and later PVC repairs. We note each change of material and its distance from the cleanout so the scope of any repair is clear.

## Why the repair is usually different

**Deformed Orangeburg: bursting or replacement, not lining.** [CIPP lining](/cipp-pipe-lining/) cures a new pipe against the inside of the old one, so it needs a host pipe that is still reasonably round and structurally continuous. Orangeburg that has gone oval or begun to collapse is a poor host: the liner would take on the flattened shape, and a deteriorating fiber wall can give way during cleaning or installation. For that pipe we recommend [pipe bursting](/pipe-bursting/), which breaks up the old line and pulls a new continuous HDPE pipe through the same path, or a conventional [sewer line replacement](/sewer-line-replacement/) where the grade also needs correcting. The rare fiber line that is still round and firm may be a lining candidate, and the camera will show whether yours is.

**Clay with roots and cracks: often lining.** Clay tile that has held its shape and grade, even with roots at every joint and a number of cracks, is often an excellent candidate for lining. After the roots are cut and the pipe is hydro-jetted clean, the liner seals every joint in one continuous tube and leaves no gaps for roots to re-enter. That was the case on a 72-foot clay line in Littleton that we lined from inside the house, and on a 78-foot terra cotta line in Denver. Clay that has a belly, a badly dropped joint or a collapsed section needs a different approach for that stretch, sometimes a spot excavation combined with lining on the rest.

## Buying a postwar home

If you are buying a house built between the late 1940s and the early 1970s, a sewer scope before closing is worth scheduling. General home inspections usually stop at the foundation, and in most Front Range cities and sanitation districts the lateral out to the main becomes the buyer's responsibility after the sale. Ask the inspector to push the camera as far toward the main as possible, and ask for the recording so a contractor can review it later. See our [FAQ](/faq/) for more about scopes and responsibility.

Postwar housing is common across [Denver](/denver/), [Jefferson County](/jefferson-county/), [Adams County](/adams-county/), [Arapahoe County](/arapahoe-county/) and [Boulder County](/boulder-county/). Whatever the neighborhood, the pipe material is something you find out by looking, not by guessing from the year the house was built.
